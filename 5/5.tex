    \begin{exercise}[Lie Factor] \hfill
        \[\text{Lie Factor} := \frac{\text{size of effect shown in graphic}}{\text{size of effect in data}}\]

        \[\text{data change} := \frac{27-12}{27} = 55.67\%\]

        \[\text{graphic change} := \frac{4cm - 1.6cm}{1.6cm} = 150\%\]

        \[\text{Lie Factor} = \frac{150}{55.67} = 2.7\]
    
        \noindent
        The Lie Factor has the value of \(2.7\) which means that the graphic is overstating.
        Therefore, it can be said, that the graphic is not representing the data correctly.
    \end{exercise}

    \begin{exercise}[Chart Junk Considered Useful?] \hfill
        \begin{itemize}
            \item[a)]
                Arguments in favour of chart junk:
                \begin{enumerate}
                    \item[I)] Embellished graphs improve long-term recall of graphed data/message.
                    \item[II)] Embellished graphs can use \emph{eye candy} to attract viewers on the importance of the graph. 
                    \item[III)] Embellished graphs may reinforce the message and takeaways for the convinced information.
                \end{enumerate}

                Arguments against chart junk:
                \begin{enumerate}
                    \item[I)] Embellished graphs may (on purpose) be subjugated to bias and introduce a message not fully supported by data.
                    \item[II)] Embellished graphs may distract the viewer from the data itself and leave room for misinterpretation.
                    \item[III)] Implementing good embellishment is hard for non illustrators, therefore, using embellishments may go wrong really quick for inexperienced designers.
                \end{enumerate}

            \item[b)] In high-risk real-time environments such as air traffic control, overdecoration and embellishments on a graph may introduce additional overhead in communication which may cause harm.

            \item[c)] In the first example, \autoref{fig:2a}, the embellishment completely distracts from the information conveyed by the graph.
            The bountiful usage of colour and 3D shape mistakenly convinces a semantic context and introduces misinterpretation and delay.

            In the second example, \autoref{fig:2b}, the graph contains a horrible colour palette which makes it hard to look at and distracts the viewer from actually interacting with the 3 to 4 messages on the graph.

    \begin{exercise}[Is ChatGPT a Good Tool for Design Feedback?]
        Yes, you could use ChatGPT for design feedback, but only in specific situations.
        ChatGPT can be useful when you need high-level design suggestions because it is good at identifying broad issues, unclear labeling, lack of hierarchy, misleading color scales or unnecessary complexity.
        It could also be helpful when you require multiple alternative design ideas quickly or need explanations of visualization guidelines, because ChatGPT can summarize design rules in a quick and understandable way (most of the time).
        
        A reason why you should not use ChatGPT is that it makes confident mistakes, because the advice sounds correct but is either irrelevant or just wrong.
        ChatGPT could also critizise aspects of a visualization that are not actually present or misinterpreted chart types.
        In conclusion ChatGPT can be helpful in some scenarios, but it is almost always better to do most things yourself.
    \end{exercise}
