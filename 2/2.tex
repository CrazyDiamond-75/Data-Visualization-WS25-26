    \begin{exercise}[Weber's Law] \hfill
    \begin{itemize}
        \item[a)]
            Its stats that the smallest detectable difference in stimulus intensity 
            (the just noticable difference or JND)
            is a constant proportion of the original stimulus intensity.
            Mathematically, it can be expressed as:

            \[\frac{\triangle I}{I} = k\]
            $I$ = original intensity of the stimulus,
            $\triangle I$ = the smallest change in intensity that can be detected (JND),
            $k$ = a constant, that depends on the type of stimulus (e.g. light, sound,...),

            So basically you could say: the stronger the original stimulus,
            the more change you need
            for a person to notice a difference.
            This applies across many senses like brightness, sound, etc.

            Fechner's law:
            This law builds on Weber's law and describes how the perceived intensity of 
            the stimulus grows as its physical intensity increases.
            It suggets that our perception of stimulus intensity is logarithmic in nature.
            Mathematically, Fechner's law can be expressed as:

            \[S = k \log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)\]
            \(S\) = perceived intensity (sensation),
            \(I\) = actual intensity of the stimulus,
            \(I_0\) = threshold intensity (the minimum intensity that can be detected),
            \(k\) = a constant that depends on the type of stimulus

            The logarithm in Fechner's law means that as the intensity of the stimulus increases,
            the perceived intensity increases at a slower rate. This reflects the idea that
            our perception of changes in stimulus intensity is not linear. Instead larger changes 
            in physical intensity are required to produce the same change in perceived intensity as
            the stimulus becomes stronger.

        Gestalt Principles:

        1. Law of proximity

        Elements that are close together are perceived as belonging together.
        In this screenshot you can see, that the navigation menu on the left groups links
        like:
        "General Information" or "Slides and podcasts" by placing them close together.
        Therefore, it is easier to navigate between these different groups.

        In the main panel, each date + announcement line are very close to each other, so they obviously 
        belong together in the category "updates".

        2. Law of Common Region

        Objects placed inside a common bounded area appear grounded.
        In this screenshot the "Lates Updates and Announcements" section is visually
        enclosed by a rounded rectangle.
        Also the sidebar on the left is cleary separated by a background panel.
        This creates strong visual grouping and improves navigation clarity.

        3. Law of similarity

        Similar elements appear to belong together.
        In this screenshot all navigation items use the same font, style and indentation.
        Furthermore all announcement entries use identical text formatting (date in bold, message)
        Because of this, Users can instantly understand which items share fuctionality
        or belong to the same type.

        4. Law of Figure-Ground 

        The brain separates foreground elements from the background.
        In this screenshot, the main course content (in white) stands out against
        the subtle grey navigation area.
        The bold title "Data Visualization WS25/26 080085" is clearly meant to be in the foreground.
        This makes the main information pop out more and improves readability.

        5. Law of Continuation

        People follow visual lines and natural reading flows.
        In this screenshot, the left navigation is a vertical list the eye naturally
        follows from top to bottom.
        The main announcement feed is also vertically aligned, encouraging smooth scrolling.
        The result is that the User can scan information efficiently without losing track.

        6. Law of Pragnanz (Simplicity)
        
        People prefer simple, organized and predictable structures.
        In this screenshot, there is a clean layout, consistent typography and simple icons.
        All content is arranged in easily digestible modules and because of this the entire interface feels 
        clean and predictable despite having many functions.
        \end{itemize}
