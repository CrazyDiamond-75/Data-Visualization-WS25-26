    \begin{exercise}[Why Visualize -- Datasaurus]
        See \lstinline|1.2.py|, \lstinline|1.2.py.out|, and \lstinline|1.2.pdf|.
        The Datasaurus dataset was created by modifying an initial dataset with wanted variance and mean by applying a fixed-point algorithm.
        On each iteration, some points are moved randomly if the transformation does not significally change the important properties.
        Additionally, a fitness is calculate which measures, how similar the dataset would be after the transformation with a certain wanted shape.
        If both fitness and important properties improve/stay similar, an iteration is accepted, and the next iteration is applied.
    \end{exercise}

    \begin{exercise}[Why Visualize -- Real Data Example] \hfill
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Specifically, each attribute is (to some degree) continuous and temporal by year. %1
            \begin{itemize}
                \item \lstinline|UNEMP|: The percentage of the unemployed over the (insured) employed. Ratio data type.
                \item \lstinline|WAGES|: Mean weekly earnings of full-time employees in shillings. Ratio data type.
                \item \lstinline|BENEFITS|: Weekly pension of adult males in a family with two children. Ratio data type.
                \item \lstinline|BWRATIO|: \lstinline|BENEFITS| over \lstinline|WAGES|---the ratio of incoming income over outgoing pension. Ratio data type.
                \item \lstinline|NNP|: The net national product---economic output measured in millions of pounds. Contextually correlating with difference between \lstinline|WAGES| and \lstinline|BENEFITS|. Interval data type (might be negative).
                \item \lstinline|DEMAND|: Ratio of \lstinline|NNP| to its future estimated value (\lstinline|NNP|\(^*\)). Ratio data type.
            \end{itemize}
            \item[2. 3. 4. 5.] See \lstinline|1.3.py| and \lstinline|1.3.45.pdf|.
            We found, that using a line chart was most fit for the dataset, as many attributes overtake others when normalizing the dataset.
            This makes it awkward to use a bar chart or area chart.
            
            As the time dimension introduces important semantics into the chart, using a scatter plot or heat map to visualize possible correlations between attributes does not take the time into account and discards important information, possibly skewing the results.

            The visualization \lstinline|1.3.45.pdf| we came up with disproves [BK79]'s hypothesis, that the insurance scheme was \emph{the} driving factor for the high unemployment in the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland, as it does not indicate a strong positive or negative correlation between \lstinline|UNEMP| and \lstinline|BWRATIO|.
            \setcounter{enumi}{5}

            \item We \emph{compare} the unemployment rate to the \lstinline|BWRATIO| value and discover a small correlation.
            \item[7. 8.] In \lstinline|1.3.7.pdf| we can see, that \lstinline|UNEMP| correlated with \lstinline|DEMAND|.
            We can see this especially during the beginning of the Great Depression in 1929, where the unemployment starts to drastically rise as the demand goes up.
            
            The Great Depression was preceded by great wealth inequality and high debt, and steadily growing \lstinline|NNP| and \lstinline|DEMAND|, but small \lstinline|WAGES|.
            Therefore, the consumers did not have enough money to satisfy the \lstinline|DEMAND|, resulting in a hard recession.
            This caused an even higher wealth inequality and sinking \lstinline|DEMAND|, leading to a decline of production and thus higher \lstinline|UNEMP|.

            In contrast to [BK79] our first visualization only shows low correlation of unemployment and the benefits to wages ratio, which suggests that high benefits play an insignificant role to the unemployment ratio.
            
            Scaling by the inverse of standard deviation and subtracting the mean value results in plots with values that are more within the same y-range and therefore easier to compare by eye.
            As opposed to normalizing the attributes by directly mapping them to a scale \([0,1]\) where \(0\) is equal to the attribute minimum, and \(1\) is equal to the attribute maximum, normalizing by the applied schema is less sensitive to outliers and better maintains the original shape of the data.
            \setcounter{enumi}{8}

            \item It might be explained due to the record birth-rate in the England\footnote{\url{https://www.bbc.com/news/magazine-16853368}}, stirring chaos and resulting in extreme above replacement-level fertility, greatly influencing the \lstinline|NNP| and predictions of such.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{exercise}
