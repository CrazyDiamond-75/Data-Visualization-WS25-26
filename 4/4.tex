    \begin{exercise}[Colors]
        I prefer the second color scheme over the others, as they have individual problems.

        The most obvious point is, that the viewer may interpret a link between \emph{Men over 60} and \emph{Women under 20}---the gradient implies continuity between groups!
        This problem does not appear in the second nor the third color scheme.
        Regarding the third color scheme, there are some additional problems to consider.
        
        Firstly, the gradients are chosen as such, that the younger men and women are not as easily seen on paper, as the older participants of the study.
        This could be interpreted as a false proportionality between age and importance to the information conveyed in the graphic.
        Secondly, there is not a big difference in between the colors inside the male and respectively female groups, which may subjugate the viewer to not perceive a difference between the subgroups in some graph charts, e.g., a multiline chart or scatter plot.
        This problem could be mitigated by choosing a more fitting chart type, e.g., a bar chart or radial tree.

        Lastly, the second color scheme offers some simplicity, which the third color scheme does not have: The individual count of different subgroups is smaller---enabling to use more contrasting colors for each subgroup.
        This is also an important point regarding the \emph{purpose} of the chart---it is supposed to highlight differences between older and younger people.
        Depending on the information to convey, the designer may benefit from using a small color scheme.
    \end{exercise}

    \begin{exercise}[Likert Scale] \hfill
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item This question matches best to the second color scheme, as from the perspective of the study participant, there may be no disagreement to the question itself.
            The interviewer asks this question really to know \emph{how} ready the participant is, which implies a sequential answer space.
            \item This question matches best to the third scale, as it could easily be used to visualize the lack of confidence in the current leadership---a sequential answer space.
            \item This question matches best to the first scale, as it comes down to the agreement or disagreement itself---a diverging answer space.
            The participant might be indifferent to the company's strategy, and the interviewer is interested in the concrete opinion, which might influence the strategy.
        \end{enumerate}
        Depending on what the interviewer really wants to convey, the color palettes for the second and third answer might also be swapped, as the results may be very similar regarding disagreement.
    \end{exercise}

    \begin{exercise}[Less Color]
        This visualization looks somewhat pretty because of the many colors used, but it is not functional at all.
        It is not easy to gather much information out of this graph.
        To fix this problem, you could group some of the categories if possible.
        You also could, instead of using many different colors, use different shades of grey or choose one color and use different luminance levels, e.g., light blue, blue, dark blue.
        Do not use too vivid colors, because they compete for attention and make it hard to focus on the data.
        Also, colors should not be the only distinguishing feature.
        You should use aspects like line thickness, transparency and labels to make the visualization
        easier to distinguishing.
    \end{exercise}

    \begin{exercise}[Color Vision Deficiency Pt. 1]
        The total probability for men to have some color vision deficiency is calculated in \autoref{eq:1}.

        \begin{IEEEeqnarray}{c}
            \label{eq:1}
            P_m = 2.45\% + 6.10\% + 0.011\% = 8.561\%
        \end{IEEEeqnarray}
        
        \noindent
        Now we want to calculate the probability, that at least one male reviewer is color blind in some way.
        We can model this with three Bernoulli random variables \(X_1, X_2, X_3\) with probability \(P_m\) for the event\footnote{\url{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWSN5kaQ1oM}}.
        We want to calculate \(\Prop(X_1 + X_2 + X_3 \ge 1)\), see \autoref{eq:2}.

        \begin{IEEEeqnarray}{lCl}
            \label{eq:2}
            \Prop(X_1 + X_2 + X_3 \ge 1) & = & 1 - \Prop(X_1 + X_2 + X_3 < 1) \\
                                         & = & 1 - \Prop(X_1 = 0 \wedge X_2 = 0 \wedge X_3 = 0) \nonumber \\
                                         & = & 1 - \Prop(X_1 = 0) \cdot \Prop(X_2 = 0) \cdot \Prop(X_3 = 0) \nonumber \\
                                         & = & 1 - (1 - 8.561\%)^3 \nonumber \\
                                         & = & 23.54\% \nonumber
        \end{IEEEeqnarray}
        
        \noindent
        Therefore we obtain the probability \(23.54\%\) that at least one of the three male reviewers has some color vision deficiency---a high enough probability for graduates to design all of their graphs color-blind-friendly.
    \end{exercise}
